## 1. McDonald's acquires Dynamic Yield (2019)

**The deal.** In March 2019, McDonald's announced it would acquire Dynamic Yield, a personalization and decision-logic technology company headquartered in New York and Tel Aviv. The deal was valued at about $300 million according to people familiar with the matter, and was reported as McDonald's largest acquisition in roughly 20 years. Sources:
- https://pulse2.com/mcdonalds-buying-dynamic-yield/ (deal value from Business Insider sources, March 26, 2019)
- https://www.eenewseurope.com/en/mcdonalds-buys-ai-startup-to-revolutionize-digital-menus/ ("largest deal in 20 years," March 26, 2019)
- https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/financing/mcdonalds-acquire-tech-company-dynamic-yield

**What it does.** Dynamic Yield's decision technology personalizes outdoor digital drive-thru menu displays using time of day, weather, current restaurant traffic, and trending menu items, and it can instantly suggest additional items based on a customer's current selections. McDonald's had tested it in several U.S. restaurants in 2018 and planned a U.S. drive-thru rollout in 2019, expanding to other top international markets afterward, with later integration into self-order kiosks and the mobile app. Sources:
- https://pulse2.com/mcdonalds-buying-dynamic-yield/
- https://www.digitalcio.in/mcdonalds-buys-dynamic-yield-to-boost-its-online-marketing-efforts/
- https://www.rabobank.com/knowledge/q011335520-mcdonalds-makes-a-big-data-move

Searches of trade and financial press confirm the acquisition, the rollout plan, and McDonald's broader tech spend, but no public source I found states a verified lift figure (e.g., "X% higher check size") tied to the Dynamic Yield integration. ## 2. The Wendy's cautionary tale (2024)

In February 2024, Wendy's CEO Kirk Tanner said on an earnings call that the chain would test "dynamic pricing" and daypart offerings on digital menu boards. Media reports framed it as "surge pricing," customers and media read it as Uber-style peak-time price hikes, boycott threats followed, and Wendy's quickly clarified it had no plans to raise prices during busy periods. Industry commentators now describe it as the case study in why customer-facing framing matters: "I don't want to become the next Wendy's," said Ashwin Kamlani, founder of dynamic pricing company Juicer, after it discontinued the product in August 2024. Sources:
- https://www.pymnts.com/news/retail/2024/wendys-insists-it-has-no-plans-to-test-surge-pricing/ (Tanner quote and Wendy's official clarification, Feb 2024)
- https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/3-restaurant-tech-fads-faded (Juicer quote and aftermath)
- https://www.govtech.com/question-of-the-day/is-wendys-going-to-introduce-surge-pricing (published Feb 28, 2024)

**Takeaway for the pitch:** Wendy's lesson is about framing, not about the idea. Their mistake was announcing price *increases* at peak demand. The Saroki's proposal is the opposite: prices stay flat or drop during peak; prices rise only on low-traffic items in slow dayparts, and any visible change is framed as a deal. Same mechanics, different headline.

## 3. Convenience-store context worth one line

Digital menu boards already let restaurants change featured items and daypart offers instantly. Per https://www.restaurantbusinessonline.com/technology/restrictions-personalized-pricing-are-coming-how-will-restaurants-be-impacted, that is the quiet, already-live version of demand-based pricing: the visible "deal" moves with the clock. The Saroki's pilot proposes the same principle in a single-store convenience setting, controlled by the owner, with no personal-data-driven pricing of any customer.
