## SLIDE 3: The problem

Static prices ignore everything your store already knows.

- The 6am commuter crowd buys coffee and breakfast sandwiches in bulk. The 2pm dead window buys almost nothing.
- Cold days spike hot coffee; hot days spike fountain drinks and cold grab-and-go.
- Your register already records all of this. Nothing in your price list reacts to it.

A price that is right at 7am is not the right price at 3pm. Convenience stores sell to changing demand with fixed numbers. That gap is margin you are leaving on the table, every day, at every location.

## SLIDE 4: The proof (McDonald's)

This is not a new idea. The biggest restaurant company in the world already paid for it.

- In March 2019, McDonald's acquired Dynamic Yield, a personalization and decision-logic company, for a reported $300 million, its largest deal in roughly 20 years. (Sources: https://pulse2.com/mcdonalds-buying-dynamic-yield/ and https://www.eenewseurope.com/en/mcdonalds-buys-ai-startup-to-revolutionize-digital-menus/)
- The technology personalizes digital drive-thru menus by time of day, weather, current traffic, and trending items, and suggests add-ons based on the current order. (Sources: https://www.digitalcio.in/mcdonalds-buys-dynamic-yield-to-boost-its-online-marketing-efforts/ and https://www.rabobank.com/knowledge/q011335520-mcdonalds-makes-a-big-data-move)
- McDonald's did not spend $300M on a hunch. It tested the technology in U.S. restaurants in 2018 before buying it.

One honest note: McDonald's never published a specific sales-lift number from this program, so we do not claim one. What the purchase proves is that demand-based menu pricing passed a real company's real pilot at real scale.

## SLIDE 5: The cautionary tale (Wendy's, 2024)

In 2024, Wendy's announced "dynamic pricing" and the internet heard "surge pricing." Backlash, boycott threats, and a retraction within days. (Source: https://www.pymnts.com/news/retail/2024/wendys-insists-it-has-no-plans-to-test-surge-pricing/)

Their mistake was the framing, not the idea. They talked about raising prices when demand is highest. Our pilot does the opposite:

- Prices never rise during peak. Peak prices stay exactly where they are.
- Changes happen in slow dayparts, mostly as small, temporary deals that move stale demand.
- Customers see "deals," never "surge." The receipt-level prices stay familiar.

Rule of the pilot: no customer ever pays more for the same item at rush hour than they did before the pilot.

## SLIDE 6: The pilot (what changes day to day)

One store. 10 Mile & Dequindre. 90 days. A handful of high-volume items, coffee, fountain drinks, breakfast sandwiches, and a rotating shelf snack, get small, pre-approved price moves based on conditions the store already sees:

- Slow window (e.g. 2 to 5pm): temporary $0.10 to $0.25 deals to lift traffic, the digital-menu-board equivalent of happy hour.
- Weather events (cold snap, heat wave): shift featured items and nudge prices on what is suddenly hot, small moves within a guardrail of [PLACEHOLDER: e.g. plus or minus $0.30].
- School and local calendar: Warren school days and local events that predictably change foot traffic.
- Every change has an owner-approved floor and ceiling. Nothing moves without your sign-off on the guardrails first.
